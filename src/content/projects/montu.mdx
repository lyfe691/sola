# Montu, a journal of Swiss mountain tours

[montu.ch](https://montu.ch) collects a friend's mountain tours in Switzerland, summer and winter, each written up in German with his own photographs. I built the site for him, and he publishes new tours on his own, from a content editor in his browser.

## What he wanted

He wanted a place to keep his tours the way he remembers them. The site says it in its own words: the technical details are only «sanfte Wegweiser» (gentle signposts), and the whole thing is «mehr Tagebuch als Führer», more diary than guidebook. So every page leads with a photograph and his writing, and the figures follow for anyone who wants to go.

## A tour page

A tour opens on its title photograph with the kind of tour, its grade, the region, the height range and the date. His introduction comes next, then the key facts, the profile and the map, and then the stages, one after another down the page.

## The stages

Each stage has its own photos to page through, each with a caption, next to its own figures and text. Where part of a route can be skipped, for example by taking a cable car, the stage ends with the alternative in an orange note. A tour can end with links to the huts, lifts and other places along the route.

## Map and elevation profile from one GPS file

A GPS watch or a phone app records a tour as a GPX file: the route as a list of points, each with its altitude. He uploads that one file to the tour. The page draws the route on a terrain map, with a green pin at the start and an orange one at the end, and plots the elevation profile from the same points. The profile picks its own scale, so a short ski tour and a long crossing both read cleanly, and pointing along it reads out the height.

Grades follow the Swiss Alpine Club's scales for hiking, ski and snowshoe tours. Hovering over or tapping the grade spells it out, names the scale and links to a description of it.

## Design

The headings are set in a serif, the text in a quiet sans, and the photographs run large with rounded corners. The menu is a small glass panel in the corner that names the section you're in, and it holds the switch between light, dark and the system's setting.

## What he edits himself

The site is built with Next.js, and everything a visitor reads lives in Sanity, a content editor built into the site. A tour is one form, split into tabs from the general details to the route and the logistics. The grade is picked from a list rather than typed, so the label on the page always matches the scale, and a stage only asks for an alternative once he marks it as skippable.

The home page is edited the same way, from the slideshow photographs to the text about the project. When he publishes, the site picks up the change on its own, without a developer.
